# Homepage Copy Proposals — June 2026

Five complete homepage copy decks for beta.highlighter.com, each making a different strategic bet.
Research basis: full product audit of the codebase, critique of the current live page, in-repo
positioning language, and competitive landscape (Readwise/Matter/Glasp, Substack/Ghost,
Damus/Primal, plus the Pocket/Omnivore shutdowns).

**Diagnosis of the current page** ("A book club that doesn't get lost in the group chat."):
well-voiced mood piece that assumes the sale — the differentiating half of the headline is
visually muted, the meta description is protocol jargon ("Nostr-native reading rooms…"),
there's no proof, key objections go unanswered (do friends need accounts? is there an app?
what happens when I click?), and the bookish register excludes podcast/article clubs.

---

# Proposal A — "The Closer"

**Bet:** the pain positioning is right; the page just never closes. Keep the group-chat-failure
hook, turn the volume up, and spend the page answering every objection.

## Meta

**Meta title:** Highlighter — Private rooms for reading together

**Meta description:** Pick a book, podcast, or article. Invite six to fifteen friends with one link. Highlight passages and talk about them right where they happen — nothing gets buried in a chat scroll.

## Hero

**H1:** Highlight a passage. Talk about it right there.

**Subheadline:** Highlighter is a private room for your book club, podcast club, or article club — six to fifteen people, invite-only. Every comment attaches to the passage it's about, so the conversation never disappears into a chat scroll.

**Primary CTA:** Start a room
*Micro-copy under button:* Takes about a minute. Friends join with one link.

**Secondary CTA:** See how a room works ↓

## Section 1 — The Problem

**Header: The group chat is where reading clubs go to die.**

You know how it goes. Someone posts a quote at 9pm with "page 134, WOW." Three people reply. By morning it's buried under weekend plans and a dog photo. A week later someone asks "wait, what chapter are we on?" and nobody answers, because the chat made the whole thing feel like homework.

The problem was never your friends. It's that a chat thread has no memory of *where* in the book — or the episode, or the article — anything happened. The conversation and the thing you're discussing live in two different places.

Highlighter puts them in the same place.

## Section 2 — The Core Idea

**Header: Comments live on the passage, not in a scroll.**

In a Highlighter room, you don't quote a passage into a chat. You highlight it — and your friends' replies attach to that exact spot. Open the book to chapter six and you'll see what everyone said about chapter six, right where they said it.

Come back two weeks later and nothing is lost. Join the room late and you can read your way *into* the conversation instead of scrolling up through 400 messages trying to reconstruct it.

There's no feed deciding what you see. No algorithm. No followers, no public profiles, no strangers. Just your people and the thing you chose to read together.

*Caption (UI screenshot):* A highlight in chapter six, with three replies anchored to it — exactly where you'll find them next month.

## Section 3 — Not Just Books

**Header: Books, podcasts, articles, videos. If it's long, it works.**

A room forms around anything long-form:

- **A book** — the classic club, minus the chaos.
- **A podcast episode** — highlight the moment at 43:12 that made you pull over, and argue about it with timestamps instead of vague memories.
- **An article or essay** — the 9,000-word piece everyone said they'd read. Now there's proof.
- **A video or even a voice note** — if your group can listen to it or read it, you can mark it up together.

Your "book club" was never really about books. It was about thinking through something with people you like. Highlighter is built for that.

## Section 4 — Physical Books and Your Library

**Header: Reading the paper copy? Point your camera at the page.**

The iOS app turns a physical book into something your group can mark up. Snap a photo of the page, and the text is extracted automatically and becomes a highlight — anchored, commentable, part of the room like any other. No retyping a paragraph with your thumbs.

**Sub-header: Everything you highlight, you keep.**

Every highlight you make or save lands in your personal library — and it stays with you across every room, long after a club wraps up. Finish three books with three different groups and you have one growing collection of the passages that mattered to you, with the conversations still attached.

## Section 5 — How It Works

**Header: From zero to first highlight in four steps.**

**1. Start a room.** Name it and pick what you're reading or listening to — a book, an episode, an article.

**2. Send one link.** Your friends tap it and they're in. No technical setup, no jargon, nothing to figure out.

**3. Read and highlight.** On your phone, in the app, or straight from a paper book with the camera. Mark what strikes you.

**4. Talk where it happened.** Reply to each other's highlights. The discussion stays pinned to the passage — this week, next month, whenever you get to chapter nine.

*CTA under steps:* **Start a room** — your first invite link is one minute away.

## Section 6 — Questions You're Probably Asking

**Header: Fair questions, straight answers.**

**Do my friends need to be technical to join?**
No. You send one invite link, they tap it, they're in the room. Nobody needs to understand anything beyond "click this."

**Is there an app?**
Yes — an iOS app, and it's where the camera capture for physical books lives. The room itself, the highlights, and the conversation all travel with your account.

**What actually happens when I click "Start a room"?**
You name the room, pick what your group is reading or listening to, and get an invite link to share. That's the whole setup.

**How big can a room be?**
Six to fifteen people. Big enough for real disagreement, small enough that everyone actually talks.

**Will strangers see what we write?**
No. Rooms are invite-only. There's no feed, no public profiles, no followers — nothing you write is performing for an audience.

**What happens to our rooms if Highlighter the company goes away?**
They survive. Highlighter is built on an open protocol called Nostr, which means your identity, your rooms, and your highlights are portable — they belong to you, not to our servers. You'll never get an email saying your reading history is being deleted in 30 days.

**Do we need to know anything about crypto?**
No. Nothing about Highlighter requires it.

## Final CTA Block

**Header: Your group already wants to do this. They just need the link.**

You've got the text thread that keeps saying "we should read something together." This is the move: pick the book — or the episode, or the article — start the room, and send one link.

**Primary CTA:** Start a room
*Micro-copy:* About a minute to set up. Friends join with a single link.

**Secondary CTA:** Get the iOS app

## Alternate H1 Options

- **"The group chat is a terrible place to discuss a book."** — Leads with the shared, instantly recognizable pain and a contrarian jolt — strongest for cold traffic, though it inherits the bookish framing the subhead would need to immediately widen.
- **"A private room for whatever your group reads — or listens to — together."** — Pure category clarity. Sacrifices punch for instant comprehension, and bakes in the podcast/article expansion at the headline level.

## Strategic Summary

This page bets that Highlighter's conversion problem isn't awareness of the pain (everyone has watched a group-chat book club die) but unanswered friction questions — so it keeps the proven pain-point hook while spending most of its real estate closing: one-link invites, the iOS camera capture, exactly what "Start a room" does, and why the rooms outlive the company. It deliberately widens the category from "book club" to any long-form club (podcasts, articles, videos) in the subhead and a dedicated section, betting that the bookish framing of the old headline was silently excluding half the addressable groups. The register throughout is a smart friend explaining a good idea plainly — concrete mechanics over literary mood.

# Proposal B — "The Permanent Library"

**Bet:** the deepest motivation is the reading LIFE, not the reading group. Pocket and Omnivore
died and took people's saves with them; permanence is the unclaimed positioning. Rooms support
the story; the library is the spine.

## Meta

**Meta title:** Highlighter — A permanent home for everything you underline

**Meta description:** Keep every passage you've ever highlighted — from books, articles, podcasts, and videos — in a library that lasts. Then read alongside a few friends in a private room.

## Hero

**H1:** A permanent home for everything you underline.

**Subheadline:** Highlighter keeps every passage you mark — in books, articles, podcasts, videos — in a personal library built to outlast any app. And it gives you a quiet room where a few friends can read alongside you.

**Primary CTA:** Start your library

**Secondary CTA:** See how a reading room works ↓

## Section 1 — The lines you underline are the truest record of your mind

Not your posts. Not your photos. The passages you stopped and marked — those are the sentences that changed how you think.

For two decades, readers have trusted that record to apps. Pocket shut down in 2025 and deleted everyone's saves. Omnivore closed the year before. Each time, years of careful reading reduced to a zip file in a goodbye email — if you saw the email in time.

The record of your mind deserves a better fate than an export folder you'll never open.

*Micro-copy:* Highlighter was built so this never happens to your highlights. Here's how.

## Section 2 — A library that only grows

Every highlight you make — and every one you save from someone else — lands in your personal library. It accumulates across every room you've ever joined, every book you've ever finished, every podcast you marked at minute 43.

Come back in ten years and it's all still there: searchable, yours, intact. The passages stack into something old readers would recognize — a commonplace book. Except this one writes itself while you read.

*Caption (under library screenshot):* Your library, three rooms and two years deep.

## Section 3 — Reading is better with six friends than six thousand followers

A room on Highlighter holds six to fifteen people, invite-only. The room picks something long-form — a novel, a podcast episode, a 9,000-word profile, a lecture — and everyone reads it together.

When something strikes you, highlight it. When you have something to say, your comment attaches to the highlight itself — anchored to the exact passage, where the next reader will find it. Nothing scrolls away. A great observation about chapter four lives on chapter four, not 300 messages up in a group chat.

No feed. No algorithm. No followers. Just the text, the people, and the margins between them.

*Micro-copy (under room screenshot):* Marta highlighted a paragraph on page 112. Four replies, all still attached to page 112.

## Section 4 — Your paper books are invited too

Some of your best reading happens with a pencil and a physical book. The Highlighter iOS app captures the page with your camera, lifts the text, and files the passage into your library — same as a highlight made on screen.

Underline in the margin on Sunday, find it in your library on Monday, discuss it with your room on Tuesday.

*Caption (under capture demo):* Point. Capture. The passage joins your library in seconds.

## Section 5 — "Permanent" is a big word. Here's exactly what we mean.

Most apps keep your data in their database. When the app goes, so does the database. We built Highlighter the other way around.

Your identity, your highlights, and your rooms live on Nostr — an open network that exists independently of our company. Highlighter is one way to read them; it isn't the only way. If we disappeared tomorrow, your library wouldn't. Another app could open it the same afternoon, with nothing exported and nothing lost.

You don't need to understand any of that to use Highlighter, and your friends don't either. They tap an invite link and start reading. The plumbing stays where plumbing belongs — out of sight, holding everything up.

*Micro-copy:* No crypto, no wallets, no setup. An invite link is the whole onboarding.

## Section 6 — What a year of reading this way looks like

Four books with your oldest friends. A podcast feed your room actually keeps up with, because someone always marks the minute worth hearing. An essay that sparked forty marginal notes. And underneath all of it, a library quietly growing — every line you found worth keeping, kept.

Most apps want your attention. This one wants to still be useful to you in thirty years.

## Final CTA Block

**Header:** Start the library you'll still have in thirty years.

**Body:** Make your first highlight, or open a room and invite the friends you've always meant to read with. Either way, everything you mark from today on is yours for good.

**Primary CTA:** Start your library

**Secondary CTA:** Download the iOS app

*Micro-copy:* Free during beta. Rooms are invite-only — you choose who's in.

## Alternate H1 Options

- **"Everything you've ever underlined, kept for life."** — Leads with the accumulation and the promise in seven words; "kept for life" carries permanence without naming a threat.
- **"Build the library no app can take down with it."** — Sharper and more competitive; directly converts the Pocket/Omnivore moment into a benefit, at the cost of being slightly more negative in register.

## Strategic Summary

This page bets that the deepest motivation isn't joining a book club — it's the quiet fear, recently proven justified by Pocket and Omnivore, that the most personal record of your intellectual life can vanish with someone else's shutdown notice. So permanence is the spine: the hero sells a lifelong library, the social rooms are positioned as the best way to fill it, and the open protocol appears exactly once, translated into a single concrete consequence ("if we disappeared tomorrow, your library wouldn't"). The register stays warm and proud rather than fearful, claiming the ownership-as-permanence angle that neither Readwise (retention) nor other Nostr apps (censorship fear) currently occupy.

# Proposal C — "The Margin"

**Bet:** don't sell a chat-logistics fix — claim an idea with five centuries of legitimacy:
conversation about a text belongs in the margin, attached to the words. Highlighter is the
first shared margin.

## Meta

**Meta title:** Highlighter — A shared margin for reading with friends

**Meta description:** A private reading room for six to fifteen people. Highlight a book, podcast, or essay, and talk about it in the margin — anchored to the exact passage, findable forever.

## Hero

**H1:** The conversation belongs in the margin.

**Subheadline:** Highlighter is a private reading room for you and a few friends. Highlight a passage in a book, podcast, article, or video — and the conversation happens right there, attached to the words, where anyone can find it a year later.

**Primary CTA:** Start a reading room
**Secondary CTA:** See how a room works

*Hero caption (under product visual):* A highlighted passage from chapter four. Three comments, anchored to it. Still exactly where they were left.

## Section 1 — The idea

**Header: Readers have argued in margins for five hundred years. The margins just never talked back.**

Marginalia is the oldest form of conversation about text. A note next to a sentence — to yourself, to the next reader, to the author who can't hear you. It works because it lives *at the passage*, not somewhere else.

Then reading went digital, and the conversation moved out of the book — into group chats, threads, and feeds, where a sharp observation about page 212 sits between a meme and someone's flight delay.

Highlighter puts the conversation back where it belongs: in the margin. And it does the one thing paper never could — it lets a few friends share the same one.

*Micro-copy:* One artifact. One shared margin. Your people in it.

## Section 2 — How it actually works

**Header: Comments stick to the passage, not the scroll.**

In a Highlighter room, you don't talk *about* the book. You talk *in* it.

- **Highlight a passage.** Your friends see it in context, exactly where you marked it.
- **Comment on the highlight.** The reply attaches to those words — permanently. When someone reads that chapter next month, the conversation is waiting there, not buried under three weeks of chat.
- **It's not just text.** Podcasts and videos work the same way: highlights anchor to timestamps, so "wait, what did she mean at 42:17" becomes a marked moment everyone can return to.
- **Physical books too.** Point the iOS app's camera at a printed page and it captures the passage. Your paperback gets a shared margin.

*Caption under timestamp visual:* A highlight at 42:17 of a podcast episode. The argument about it lives at 42:17, forever.

## Section 3 — A room, not a network

**Header: Six to fifteen people. No feed. No followers. Nothing to perform.**

A room is invite-only and intentionally small — the size of a good dinner table. The group picks something long-form, and everyone reads, listens, or watches at their own pace, leaving highlights as they go.

There's no algorithm deciding what you see, no follower count to grow, no public square to play to. The book is the hero. The interface gets out of the way. The only thing competing for attention is the text itself.

*Micro-copy:* Send one invite link. That's the entire onboarding.

## Section 4 — "Why not just use our group chat?"

**Header: Your group chat is where good observations go to die.**

You've tried this already. Someone posts a quote, two people react, someone changes the subject, and by Thursday the thread is about dinner plans. The insight isn't gone — it's worse than gone. It's on page 4,000 of the scroll, unfindable, unattached to anything.

Chat is built for *now*. A margin is built for *the text*. In Highlighter, a comment on chapter four stays on chapter four. Join a room late, read at your own speed, and the conversation meets you at each passage as you arrive — instead of spoiling ahead or vanishing behind you.

Keep the group chat. Just stop asking it to hold your best thinking.

## Section 5 — The margin you keep

**Header: Every highlight becomes part of your library.**

Each passage you mark — and each one you save from a friend's highlight — accumulates in a personal library that travels with you across every room you join. Finish a book with a room this spring, and years from now you can still pull up the exact lines that mattered, with the conversation that happened around them.

You're not just reading together. You're building a record of how you read.

## Section 6 — Yours, durably

**Header: Built to outlast us.**

Marginalia survives because it lives in the book, not in a company's database. Highlighter is built the same way: on an open protocol, so your identity, your rooms, and your highlights are portable and independent of us. If Highlighter disappeared tomorrow, your margins wouldn't.

That's not a feature most apps lead with. But if you're going to put years of reading into something, it should be the first thing you ask.

## Final CTA Block

**Header: Pick the book. Send the link.**

Choose something worth arguing about, invite five to fifteen people, and start marking it up. The first good comment anchored to the right passage will make the case better than we can.

**Primary CTA:** Start a reading room
**Secondary CTA:** Get the iOS app

*Micro-copy:* Free during beta. One invite link is all your friends need.

## Alternate H1 Options

- **"Five hundred years of arguing in margins. Finally, a shared one."** — Leads with the historical weight of marginalia and lands the product's single novel act — sharing the margin — in one move; riskier length, but does the full concept-to-product job inside the headline itself.
- **"Read together. Talk in the text, not about it."** — Trades the marginalia metaphor for its plainest mechanical truth — conversation located inside the work — which grounds visitors who'd find "margin" abstract before seeing the product.

## Strategic Summary

This page bets that Highlighter wins not by fixing a logistics problem (chat scroll) but by claiming an idea: conversation about a text belongs physically attached to the text, a practice with five centuries of legitimacy that no digital product has made shared. Every section converts that concept into observable product behavior — anchored comments, timestamped highlights, camera-captured pages — so the philosophy never floats free of the mechanism. The intended hero reaction is recognition rather than persuasion: "margins were always the right place for this; of course someone finally made one you can share."

# Proposal D — "The Ritual"

**Bet:** the buyer is homesick for a ritual — thinking alongside people they like, on a rhythm.
Sell belonging; let features appear only as proof the ritual will hold.

## Meta

**Meta title:** Highlighter — A private reading room for you and your friends

**Meta description:** Pick a book, a podcast, or a long article. Invite a few people you like. Read it together, highlight as you go, and find each other in the margins.

## Hero

**H1:** Read with your people again.

**Subheadline:** Highlighter is a private reading room for you and a handful of friends. Pick something long — a book, a podcast, an article — and meet in the margins: their highlights wait for you, your notes wait for them.

**Primary CTA:** Start your reading room

**Secondary CTA:** See how a room works

*Micro-copy under CTA:* Invite-only. Six to fifteen people. No feed, no strangers.

## Section 1 — The feeling

**Header: You didn't stop loving books. You stopped having anyone to read them with.**

Somewhere between the group chat and the feed, reading became a solitary habit again. You finish a chapter that deserves a conversation, and there's nowhere to have it. The book club fizzled. The podcast you'd love to argue about goes undiscussed. The internet offers you ten thousand strangers' takes and none of your friends'.

Highlighter is for the smaller, older thing: a few people you actually like, reading the same thing at the same time, leaving marks for each other along the way.

## Section 2 — The room

**Header: One room. One thing to read. The people you chose.**

A room on Highlighter holds six to fifteen people and one piece of long-form — a novel, a podcast episode, a longread, a video. Everyone makes their way through it on their own time. As you read, you highlight. When something deserves a comment, you leave it *on the highlight itself* — anchored to the exact passage, not buried four hundred messages up in a chat.

So when your friend reaches page 212, your note is sitting right there in the margin, waiting. That's the whole trick. Conversation that lives where the idea lives.

*Caption under product screenshot:* A comment pinned to the sentence that started it — still there next week, still there next year.

## Section 3 — Not just books (objection handling)

**Header: "My friends won't download another app." They don't have to start with one.**

Two worries kill every reading group before it starts. Here's how Highlighter handles both:

**"Getting everyone set up is a chore."** You send one invite link. Your friends tap it, and they're in the room. That's the entire onboarding for them — no public profile to build, no followers to acquire, nothing to perform.

**"Not everyone in my group reads books."** Good — neither does every room. Rooms work just as well around a podcast episode, a long article, or a video. Some groups do a book a month; some do an essay a week; some just pass around whatever one person can't stop thinking about. The format is yours.

And if your book is a physical one, the iOS app captures the page with your camera and turns it into a highlight you can mark up and share.

## Section 4 — Your first week

**Header: Your first week on Highlighter**

**Day 1 — Open a room.** Name it, pick the first thing you'll read together. A short story or a single podcast episode is a perfect opening move — finishable, discussable.

**Day 1, five minutes later — Send the link.** Text it to the five or ten people you wish you talked to about ideas more often. One link, one tap, they're in.

**Days 2–6 — Read, and leave a trail.** Highlight what stops you. Comment on a friend's highlight when you reach it. You'll notice the small pleasure quickly: arriving at a passage and finding someone already there.

**Day 7 — Pick the next one.** This is where it becomes a ritual instead of an app. Somebody nominates the next read, and now there's always something your circle is in the middle of together.

*Micro-copy:* Most rooms find their rhythm by the second pick.

## Section 5 — Quiet by design

**Header: No feed. No followers. No one watching.**

Highlighter has no algorithm deciding what you see, no public profiles, no follower counts, no strangers. Nothing on the page is engineered to keep you scrolling — there's nothing to scroll. There is only the thing your group is reading and the marks your people have made on it.

It's built on Nostr, an open protocol, which means your rooms and your identity belong to you — portable, and independent of us as a company. You're not building a circle inside someone's walled garden. You're building one you can keep.

## Section 6 — Your library

**Header: Everything you've ever underlined, still yours.**

Every highlight you make — in any room, from any book, episode, or article, including pages captured from paper books — collects into your personal library. Years from now, you'll be able to return to the exact sentence that mattered, and the conversation your friends had around it.

A feed forgets you by Thursday. A library is a record of how you and your people have been thinking, together, over time.

## Final CTA Block

**Header: Pick the first thing. We'll hold the room.**

You already know who belongs in it — the friend who texts you paragraphs, the one who always asks what you're reading, the three people from the old book club. Choose something worth their time and send the link.

**Primary CTA:** Start your reading room

**Secondary CTA:** Get the iOS app

*Micro-copy:* Free to start. One link to invite everyone.

## Alternate H1 Options

- **"Their underlines are waiting for you."** — Leads with the product's most distinctive emotional moment — arriving at a passage your friend already marked — making the mechanism itself feel like belonging.
- **"A reading room for the people you'd rather think with."** — Names the category ("reading room") for instant comprehension while flattering the visitor's circle, positioning the product as a place rather than a tool.

## Strategic Summary

This page bets that the buyer isn't shopping for annotation software — they're homesick for a ritual of thinking alongside people they like, and they'll join the product that promises that belonging back. So every section sells the circle first (your people, your rhythm, your margins) and lets the features — anchored comments, one-link invites, podcast and article support, OCR capture, the portable library — appear only as proof that the ritual will actually hold. The conversion mechanics follow the same logic: the CTA asks you to start a *room*, not an account, and the "first week" section converts aspiration into a concrete, low-stakes opening move.

# Proposal E — "The Mechanic"

**Bet:** the anchored-comment mechanic is so legible that demonstrating it beats any category
framing. No category words in the H1; every section is a checkable behavior staged as a
concrete scene.

**Meta title:** Highlighter — Highlight a passage. Your friends reply on it.

Reading a physical book? Open the iOS app, point the camera at the page, and the text is extracted automatically. Swipe over the lines you loved. Your paperback highlight lands in the room next to everyone else's, anchored and quotable.

Highlights don't expire when the room moves on. Everything you mark — and everything you save from others — accumulates in one personal library across all your rooms. Six months from now, you can pull up every passage you kept from that one book, including what your friends said about each one.
